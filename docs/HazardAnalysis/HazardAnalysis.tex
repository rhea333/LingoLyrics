\section{Introduction}

This document is the hazard analysis for \progname{}, a language learning product based on songs. It is made up of a mobile learner application and a song map editor that runs in a web browser. Learners study synchronized, annotated lyrics on
their phone and send selected vocabulary to Anki. Creators use the editor to
prepare the song maps that drive each lesson.

\progname{} is not a system on which safety critically depends: it
controls no machinery and cannot directly injure anyone. It is, however, used by
the general public, often with headphones and on the move, and it writes into
the learner's own Anki collection. Its hazards are therefore mostly about
hearing and attention, incorrect learning content, loss or corruption of user
data, privacy, legal exposure from song content, and the learner's wellbeing.

\subsection*{Definition of Hazard}

Following Nancy Leveson's work~\cite{Leveson2011}, a \textbf{hazard} is a
property or condition of \progname{} that, together with a condition in its
environment, can lead to a \textbf{loss}. A \textbf{loss} is any outcome that a stakeholder would find
unacceptable. The losses considered in this document are listed in
Section~\ref{SecScope}.

\subsection*{Intended Audience}

This document is written for the \progname{} team, who will implement the
requirements it produces, for the teaching staff and peer reviewers who
assess it, and for anyone who later extends the product and needs to know
which risks have already been considered.

\subsection*{Changes to the Template}

The template has been followed with five additions. Section~\ref{SecBoundary}

\section{Scope and Purpose of Hazard Analysis} \label{SecScope}

The purpose of this analysis is to identify the ways the components of
\progname{} can fail or be misused, the effects and causes of those failures,
and the requirements or other mitigations that reduce each risk to an
acceptable level. Requirements discovered here are listed in
Section~\ref{SecRequirements} and are included in the Software Requirements
Specification (SRS).

The analysis covers both failures (the system does something wrong) and hazards
that exist when the system works as designed (for example, a correctly
synchronized lesson that distracts a learner who is walking in traffic).

The losses that the hazards in this document could lead to are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{L1 Physical harm:} hearing discomfort or damage from
        unexpected audio, injury from distraction while moving, or discomfort
        for photosensitive users from flashing highlights.
  \item \textbf{L2 Incorrect learning:} the learner learns a wrong meaning,
        reading, pronunciation, or word boundary, or unknowingly learns an
        offensive term, and carries that error into real conversations and
        into their Anki reviews.
  \item \textbf{L3 Loss or corruption of user data:} notes in the learner's
        Anki collection are duplicated, malformed, or overwritten, selected
        vocabulary is silently lost, or a creator loses timing and annotation
        work.
  \item \textbf{L4 Privacy and security:} personal data (including the
        device's IP address) leaves the device without consent, or a malicious
        song map or misconfigured integration allows an attacker to run code or
        change the learner's data.
  \item \textbf{L5 Legal:} distributing lyrics or recordings without the
        right to do so.
  \item \textbf{L6 Wellbeing:} the product causes stress, discouragement, or
        compulsive use, for example through manipulative engagement
        mechanics.
\end{itemize}

The system under analysis consists of the following components, which are
under the team's control:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Lesson Player} (learner app): plays the song's audio and
        highlights the current lyric line and word using the song map's
        timestamps.
  \item \textbf{Lyric Display and Language Support} (learner app): renders
        lyrics in their original script, the stored word segmentation, and the
        stored transliteration.
  \item \textbf{Annotation and Lesson Content}: translations, contextual
        annotations, and content metadata contained in each song map and
        shown to the learner.
  \item \textbf{Song Map Format and Validator}: the shared JSON Schema and the
        validation code used by both the editor (on export) and the learner
        app (on import).
  \item \textbf{Song Map Editor} (web): the creator's tool for aligning timing,
        entering translations and annotations, verifying segmentation and
        transliteration, and exporting song maps.
  \item \textbf{Anki Handoff}: the learner app's module that sends a selected
        vocabulary item to Anki through the AnkiDroid API~\cite{AnkiDroidAPI},
        AnkiConnect~\cite{AnkiConnect}, or an export file (the method is decided by the Proof of Concept, risk R3 in
        the Development Plan).
  \item \textbf{Local Data Storage} (learner app): stored lessons, display
        preferences, and the queue of vocabulary waiting to be sent to Anki.
\end{enumerate}

The following elements are outside the team's control but are still critical
to the system, so they are included as sources of failure where relevant:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the learner's phone and operating system (iOS or Android), including
        system volume, audio routing, and headphones,
  \item the creator's computer and web browser,
  \item Anki, AnkiDroid, and the AnkiConnect extension, and the learner's Anki
        collection,
  \item the audio and lyric content itself and the rights attached to it,
  \item the libraries from other vendors that the app and the editor are
        built with.
\end{itemize}

\section{Critical Assumptions} \label{SecAssumptions}

The team has kept assumptions to a minimum. In particular, song maps are
\emph{not} assumed to be free of errors, song map files are \emph{not}
assumed to come from a trusted source, and learners are \emph{not} assumed to
use the app only while stationary. All three are treated as hazards in
Section~\ref{SecFMEA}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[CA1:] Software from other vendors (the phone operating system, the web
        browser, Anki, AnkiDroid, and AnkiConnect) behaves as described in its
        public documentation. Failures caused by defects inside that software
        are out of scope, although the app must still detect and report a
        failed handoff (H6.4).
  \item[CA2:] The learner's phone meets the minimum operating system version
        supported by the Expo SDK release the app is built with.
  \item[CA3:] In the initial release, the team members who create and review
        lessons act in good faith. They may make mistakes, which are treated
        as hazards (H3.1, H3.2, H5.2, H5.4), but they do not deliberately
        enter false reviewer names or licence information. This matters
        because IN5 and LG1 rely on names and licences entered by people and
        cannot verify them without user accounts. Song maps from anyone
        outside the team are still treated as untrusted (H4.3 to H4.5).
\end{enumerate}

Each assumption will be checked again when the scope changes. CA3 in
particular no longer holds once creators outside the team can publish
lessons, and the hazards it covers would then need verified identities.

\section{Failure Mode and Effect Analysis} \label{SecFMEA}

A Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA) was used to identify and analyze
hazards component by component. Each row lists a failure mode, its effects
(with the related loss from Section~\ref{SecScope}), its causes, how the
failure is detected, the recommended action, its ratings for severity,
occurrence, and detection with the resulting risk priority number, and the
safety or security requirements (Section~\ref{SecRequirements}) that mitigate
it. Each
row has a reference number (\emph{Ref.}) so that requirements can trace back
to it.

\subsection{How the Analysis Was Carried Out}

FMEA was chosen because \progname{} is made of a small number of components
with clear responsibilities (Section~\ref{SecBoundary}), and the requirements
are still being written. Working through one component at a time makes it
easy to see which component a requirement belongs to. A top down method such
as fault tree analysis suits a system with one or two catastrophic top events,
which \progname{} does not have. Its losses (L1 to L6) are many and mostly
independent, so a bottom up method finds more of them.

The analysis followed these steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For each component, the team listed what it is responsible for and
        asked the following questions about each responsibility: What if it
        is not done? What if it is done wrongly, too early, or too late? What
        if it is done correctly but still causes harm? How could someone
        misuse it on purpose?
  \item Each answer that could lead to a loss in Section~\ref{SecScope} was
        recorded as a failure mode, with its effects and causes.
  \item Each input and output in Table~\ref{TblInterfaces} was checked to
        make sure it appears in at least one failure mode.
  \item The course checklist's prompt to think broadly about safety was used
        to look for security, privacy, legal, and wellbeing hazards that the
        first pass had missed. Rows such as H1.4, H2.4, H3.2, H3.3, and H6.5
        come from this step.
  \item Each failure mode was rated using Table~\ref{TblScales}, a recommended
        action was written for it, and that action was turned into one or more
        requirements that can be tested.
  \item Every requirement was checked to trace to at least one failure mode,
        and every failure mode to at least one requirement.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hazards Out of Scope}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Hardware failure of the learner's phone, headphones, or the creator's
        computer.
  \item Defects inside Anki, AnkiDroid, AnkiConnect, the operating system, or
        the browser (see CA1).
  \item The overall volume the learner chooses with their device's controls.
        The app controls when playback starts and stops, how it starts, and
        reports the loudness of each track to its creator (H1.2). Limits on
        listening over long periods are left to the operating system's hearing
        protection features.
  \item Hazards of streaming service integration, a production music
        catalogue, user accounts, and cloud sync, none of which are in the
        initial release.
  \item A user deliberately modifying the installed app or the files on their
        own device.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Rating Scales}

Each failure mode is rated on three scales from 1 to 10. The ratings describe
the failure mode \emph{before} the recommended action is taken, so they show
how much each action matters. When a failure mode can lead to more than one
loss, its severity is that of the most severe loss, even if that loss is only
possible rather than likely. The detection rating describes the checks that
exist without the recommended action. Checks that are themselves part of the
recommended action do not lower it.

\begin{table}[h!]
\caption{Rating scales} \label{TblScales}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lXXX}
\toprule
\textbf{Rating} & \textbf{Severity (S)} & \textbf{Occurrence (O)} &
\textbf{Detection (D)}\\
\midrule
9 to 10 & Physical harm, including possible harm (L1), legal exposure (L5),
or a privacy or security breach (L4) & Almost certain in normal use & Cannot
be detected before it reaches the user\\
7 to 8 & Permanent loss or damage of user data (L3), or a serious privacy or
security weakness that does not by itself expose data & Frequent & Detected
only by chance\\
5 to 6 & The learner learns incorrect content (L2), a goal from the Problem
Statement fails for some users, or lasting stress or discouragement (L6) &
Occasional & Detected by careful review or targeted testing\\
3 to 4 & User data is lost but can be recovered, the user is clearly
inconvenienced, or brief discomfort (L6) & Rare & Usually detected by routine
tests\\
1 to 2 & Minor inconvenience with no lasting effect & Very unlikely & Almost
certainly detected by automated checks\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The risk priority number is $\mathit{RPN} = S \times O \times D$, which ranges
from 1 to 1000. A failure mode is \textbf{high priority} if
$\mathit{RPN} \ge 150$ or $S \ge 9$. The second condition keeps a failure
mode that could cause physical harm or legal exposure high priority even when
it is unlikely. The ratings are estimates by the team and will be reviewed
after the Proof of Concept.

\section{Safety and Security Requirements} \label{SecRequirements}

The requirements below were discovered through the FMEA. Each requirement
lists the FMEA rows it mitigates. All of them are new requirements and have been
added to the SRS with the same identifiers, where each one also has a rationale
and a fit criterion. HS1 to HS9 are under the safety critical requirements,
AC, IN, PR, AU, and IM are under the security requirements, and LG1 is under
the legal requirements. In the SRS, the numeric values used here (1 second, three
flashes per second, 60 seconds, 0.5 seconds, $-16$ LUFS, and 2 LU) are written
as the symbolic constants \texttt{DISCONNECT\_PAUSE}, \texttt{MAX\_FLASHES},
\texttt{AUTOSAVE\_INT}, \texttt{FADE\_IN}, \texttt{TARGET\_LOUDNESS}, and
\texttt{LOUDNESS\_TOL}. The values of these and of \texttt{AUDIO\_DUR\_TOL},
\texttt{MAX\_MAP\_SIZE}, and \texttt{MAX\_NESTING} are listed in SRS
Appendix A, \emph{Symbolic Constants}. The SRS also lists, for each FMEA row, the requirements
that mitigate it (SRS Appendix B, \emph{Hazards to Requirements}).

\subsection{Health and Safety Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[HS1.] The learner app shall not start audio playback without an
        explicit action by the learner. \emph{(H1.2)}
  \item[HS2.] The learner app shall pause playback within 1 second when the
        active audio output device is disconnected. \emph{(H1.2)}
  \item[HS3.] Once a lesson has been started, the learner app shall continue
        playing it to the end, with no interaction required, while the screen
        is locked or the app is in the background. The app shall show a notice
        on first launch advising against using the screen while driving,
        cycling, or crossing traffic. \emph{(H1.4)}
  \item[HS4.] Lyric highlighting shall not produce more than three large area
        flashes in any period of one second~\cite{WCAG231}, and shall follow
        the operating system's reduce motion setting. \emph{(H2.4)}
  \item[HS5.] The learner app shall indicate the active line and word with a
        cue other than colour alone, and shall respect the device's text size
        setting. \emph{(H2.5)}
  \item[HS6.] Each song map shall include an explicit content flag. Lessons
        flagged as explicit shall show a warning before opening and can be
        hidden through a learner setting. Annotations of vulgar or offensive
        terms shall include a note describing their register. \emph{(H3.2)}
  \item[HS7.] The learner app shall not use streak loss penalties, countdown
        timers, or unsolicited push notifications to encourage use, and shall
        show each lesson's difficulty level before it is opened.
        \emph{(H1.4, H3.3)}
  \item[HS8.] When playback starts or resumes, the learner app shall raise
        the playback volume from silence to its set level over 0.5 seconds.
        \emph{(H1.2)}
  \item[HS9.] The editor shall measure the integrated loudness of each
        track's audio, as defined in ITU-R BS.1770~\cite{BS1770}, and display
        it to the creator. Before export, it shall warn the creator when the
        loudness differs from the target of $-16$ LUFS by more than 2 LU. The
        measured value shall be stored in the song map. \emph{(H1.2)}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Access Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[AC1.] The learner app shall only add notes, and only to the
        LingoLyrics deck. It shall not edit or delete existing notes, decks,
        or note types in the learner's Anki collection. \emph{(H6.3)}
  \item[AC2.] If AnkiConnect is used, the product and its setup instructions
        shall allow only the specific origin the product requires, never a
        wildcard origin, and shall require AnkiConnect's API key. The setup
        instructions shall warn the learner before any step that makes
        AnkiConnect listen on an address other than \texttt{127.0.0.1}.
        \emph{(H6.5)}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Integrity Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[IN1.] The learner app shall drive lyric highlighting from the audio
        player's reported position and shall realign the highlight after
        every seek, pause, resume, and return from the background.
        \emph{(H1.1)}
  \item[IN2.] The song map validator shall check, in addition to the schema,
        the conditions of a valid song map given in the SRS
        (\emph{Formal Specification}, conditions V1 to V8): among them, lines
        are in order and do not overlap, word times fall within their line,
        all times fall within the audio duration, segmentation is verified,
        and every annotated target has both a translation and an annotation.
        The editor shall run it before export and the learner app shall run
        it on import.
        \emph{(H1.1, H4.1)}
  \item[IN3.] Each song map shall declare its format version. The learner app
        shall refuse to open a map with an unsupported major version and shall
        tell the learner why. \emph{(H4.2)}
  \item[IN4.] Song maps shall store word segmentation and transliteration
        explicitly, and the learner app shall display the stored values
        without segmenting again at runtime. The editor shall mark automatically
        generated values as unverified until the creator confirms them, and
        shall not export a map containing unverified lines.
        \emph{(H2.1, H2.2)}
  \item[IN5.] Each annotation shall record the name of its author and the
        name of its reviewer. The editor shall not mark a map as published
        while any annotation has no reviewer or has its author as its
        reviewer. \emph{(H3.1)}
  \item[IN6.] The editor shall autosave the current draft at least every 60
        seconds, offer to restore it when reopened, warn before closing with
        unsaved changes, provide undo and redo for all edits, and ask for
        confirmation before any operation that changes more than one line.
        \emph{(H5.1, H5.2)}
  \item[IN7.] The editor shall let the creator set an audio latency offset,
        either by entering it or by tapping along to a test click track, and
        shall apply it to recorded timestamps. It shall also provide a preview
        that plays the map as the learner app will. \emph{(H5.3)}
  \item[IN8.] The learner app shall report a vocabulary item as sent only
        after Anki confirms that the note was created. Items that have not
        been confirmed shall be stored on the device, kept across app
        restarts, and available to retry or export to a file.
        \emph{(H6.4, H7.1)}
  \item[IN9.] Before adding a note, the learner app shall check the
        LingoLyrics deck for an existing note with the same term and context,
        and shall disable the send control while a request is in progress.
        \emph{(H6.1)}
  \item[IN10.] The learner app shall fill Anki note fields by field name,
        shall stop with a message if the note type lacks the \emph{Term},
        \emph{Translation}, or \emph{Context} field, and shall send all text
        as UTF-8. \emph{(H6.2)}
  \item[IN11.] On import, the learner app shall check that the audio file
        referenced by a map exists, can be decoded, and has a duration within
        \texttt{AUDIO\_DUR\_TOL} of the duration recorded in the map, and
        shall reject the lesson with a message if not. \emph{(H1.3)}
  \item[IN12.] The learner app shall include fonts covering every script used
        by its supported languages, and all song maps shall be read and
        written as UTF-8. \emph{(H2.3)}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Privacy Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[PR1.] The initial release shall not send any data off the device,
        other than notes the learner explicitly sends to Anki and error reports
        the learner chooses to share (AU1). Any analytics or crash reporting
        added later shall stay off unless the learner turns it on.
        \emph{(H7.2)}
  \item[PR2.] The learner app shall request the AnkiDroid permission only when
        the learner first sends a word, with an explanation of why it is
        needed, and shall request no other sensitive permissions.
        \emph{(H6.6)}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Audit Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[AU1.] The learner app shall let a learner prepare a report of a
        suspected error on any lyric line. The report shall contain the map
        identifier, map version, line, and the learner's comment. The app
        shall pass the report to the operating system's share sheet, so that
        the learner decides whether and where to send it (for example, by
        email or to the project's issue tracker), and shall not send it
        itself. \emph{(H3.1)}
  \item[AU2.] Each song map shall record a version number and, for every
        export, the date and the creator who exported it. \emph{(H3.1, H5.2)}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Immunity Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[IM1.] The song map parser in both the editor and the learner app shall
        reject a map whose size exceeds \texttt{MAX\_MAP\_SIZE} or whose
        nesting depth exceeds \texttt{MAX\_NESTING} before parsing it fully,
        and both shall display all map text as plain text, never as HTML.
        \emph{(H4.3)}
  \item[IM2.] Song maps shall refer to audio and other resources only by
        relative paths that resolve inside the map package. The validator
        shall reject absolute paths, remote URLs, and any path that leaves the
        package, such as one containing a \texttt{..} segment. The learner app
        shall extract each imported package only into a new folder that it
        owns. \emph{(H4.4, H4.5, H7.2)}
  \item[IM3.] The project shall pin the exact version of every library from
        other vendors in a committed lock file. CI shall run an automated
        vulnerability check on every pull request and fail when a high or
        critical vulnerability is reported. Every new dependency and every
        major version change shall be reviewed by a team member other than
        the one who added it before it is merged. \emph{(H7.3)}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Legal Requirements}

\begin{itemize}
  \item[LG1.] The editor shall require source and licence information for the
        audio and lyrics before a map can be exported, and the learner app
        shall show these credits for each lesson. \emph{(H5.4)}
\end{itemize}

\section{Roadmap} \label{SecRoadmap}

The requirements in Section~\ref{SecRequirements} have been written to the
scope that the team plans to implement in the capstone timeline. Every one of
them will be implemented within that timeline, so every high priority failure
mode in Table~\ref{TblPriority} is mitigated by all of its requirements, not
only by some of them:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Capstone timeline:} HS1 to HS9, AC1, IN1 to IN12, PR1, PR2,
        AU1, AU2, IM1 to IM3, and LG1. HS7 and PR1 constrain the design rather
        than add features, so they are met by not adding engagement mechanics
        or analytics.
  \item \textbf{Depends on the Proof of Concept:} AC2 applies only if
        AnkiConnect is chosen for the Anki handoff (PoC risk R3), and will be
        implemented in the capstone timeline if it is. The detailed behaviour
        of IN8 to IN10 and PR2 will be confirmed once the handoff method is
        decided.
\end{itemize}

Three requirements were narrowed so that they can be met in the capstone
timeline. The parts removed are future work, and the risk they would have
reduced is accepted in Table~\ref{TblResidual}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{HS3} requires that a started lesson can be followed by
        listening alone, with the screen locked, and the notice on first
        launch. A separate listening mode designed for use while moving (for
        example, larger controls or spoken line translations) is future work.
        Whether it is needed will be decided from usability testing.
  \item \textbf{IN5} enforces in the editor that each annotation names a
        reviewer other than its author. Because the initial release has no
        user accounts, the names are entered by the creator and cannot be
        verified. Verified reviewer identities need accounts and are future
        work.
  \item \textbf{AU1} prepares the error report in the app and hands it to the
        operating system's share sheet. The product has no backend to receive
        reports, so delivering them automatically to creators is future work.
\end{itemize}

IN7 requires a latency offset entered by the creator or measured by tapping
along to a click track. Calibration that measures output latency without the
creator's help is not required.

H3.1 & A reviewer may miss an error. Reviewer names cannot be verified (IN5).
Reports reach creators only if learners send them (AU1). & In the initial
release, all lessons are made and reviewed by the team, so the reviewer is
known. AU2 lets a corrected map be traced.\\
H6.4 & Queued items are lost if the app is uninstalled or the device's storage
fails. & These events are outside the app's control. The learner can export
queued items to a file at any time (IN8).\\
H2.2, H2.1 & A creator may confirm a wrong segmentation or reading. & Each
value is checked by a person once (IN4). The acceptance suite is compared
against a reference checked by the team, and learners can report errors
(AU1).\\
H3.2 & A creator may fail to set the explicit content flag or the register
note. & The flag is a required field (HS6), the reviewer checks annotations
(IN5), and learners can report errors (AU1).\\
H7.3 & A new vulnerability may be published after a release, or a harmful
version may not yet be known when it is reviewed. & The lock file stops
unreviewed versions from entering a build, and the vulnerability check runs on
every pull request, so a newly reported flaw is caught at the next change
(IM3).\\
H5.4 & A creator may enter false source or licence information. The product
cannot verify it. & The initial release distributes only content that the
team has checked is created by the team, in the public domain, or licensed
(BR3 in the SRS).\\
H5.2 & A bulk operation that the creator confirmed may still be wrong. &
Undo, the confirmation, and the preview make the error visible and
reversible (IN6). AU2 records each export.\\
H5.3 & Output latency that changes during a session (for example, with
Bluetooth) is not corrected by a single offset. & The preview (IN7) shows the
error before export, and the creator can calibrate again.\\
H6.5 & The learner may change the AnkiConnect configuration themselves, for
example by allowing all origins. & The product's instructions never ask for
this (AC2), and the API key still protects the collection. Applies only if
AnkiConnect is chosen.\\
H2.4 & Flash limits are measured on the acceptance suite. A faster song may
still exceed them. & The highlight style does not change large areas (HS4),
so a fast song produces small changes rather than large flashes.\\
H7.2 & An updated dependency may add network traffic. A shared error report
contains what the learner writes. & The dependency review and network capture of
the PR1 fit criterion are repeated before each release. The learner chooses whether to share a report
and where to send it (AU1).\\
H4.5 & A defect in the operating system's file handling may still let a path
escape the folder. & Paths are checked after they are resolved, and each
package is extracted into a new empty folder (IM2), so an escape could only
reach the app's own storage.\\
H4.3 & A defect in the JSON parser library may still be exploitable. & Size
and depth limits are applied before parsing, and map text is never
interpreted as HTML (IM1), which limits what a defect can do.\\
H4.4 & A defect in the validator may accept a remote URL. & The validator rule
is tested with maps containing absolute paths and URLs (IM2), and the app has no other code that
fetches resources from the network.\\

\section{Appendix:  Reflection}

\input{../Reflection.text}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{What went well while writing this deliverable?}\\
    Writing the hazard analysis alongside the SRS worked well. Each
    recommended action became a requirement with a fit criterion in the SRS
    straight away, so the two documents grew together instead of being
    matched up at the end. Going through the system one component at a time
    also kept the work easy to divide, since each component already has an
    owner in the Development Plan. The rating scales were the other thing
    that went well. Once the scales were tied to the losses L1 to L6, most
    disagreements about a rating could be settled by pointing to a row of
    Table~\ref{TblScales} rather than arguing from opinion.

    \item \textbf{What pain points did you experience during this
    deliverable, and how did you resolve them?}\\
    The biggest pain point was rating consistently. In the first draft,
    similar failure modes had quite different scores, and some detection
    ratings were lowered by checks that were really part of the recommended
    action. The review comments pointed this out. We resolved it by writing
    down two rules next to the scales (the worst possible loss sets the
    severity, and the recommended action does not count towards detection)
    and then rating every row again from scratch.

    The second pain point was scope. Some of our first recommended actions,
    such as verified reviewer identities and automatic delivery of error
    reports, would need user accounts and a backend that the initial release
    does not have. We could not simply drop them, because the risks are real.
    We resolved this by narrowing HS3, IN5, IN7, and AU1 to what we can build
    in the capstone timeline and recording the risk we accept in
    Table~\ref{TblResidual}, so the gap is visible instead of hidden.

    Keeping the hazard analysis and the SRS in step was also tedious, since a
    change to one requirement meant editing two documents and a traceability
    table. A small script that compares the requirement identifiers and FMEA
    references in both documents made this much less error prone.

    \item \textbf{Which of your listed risks had your team thought of before
    this deliverable, and which did you think of while doing this
    deliverable? For the latter ones (ones you thought of while doing the
    Hazard Analysis), how did they come about?}\\
    Before this deliverable we had already identified the three Proof of
    Concept risks in the Development Plan: lyric sync on a phone (R1), which
    became H1.1, segmenting lyrics (R2), which became H2.1 and H2.2, and
    sending words to Anki from a phone (R3), which became the H6 rows. We had
    also discussed copyright from the start, since the Problem Statement
    limits content to material that is created by the team, in the public
    domain, or licensed (H5.4). At that point we thought of all of these as
    technical or project risks, not as harm to users.

    Most of the other rows were new. They came about in three ways.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \emph{Thinking about how the app is really used.} Picturing a
            learner with headphones on a bus or walking to class led to
            sudden loud audio (H1.2), distraction while moving (H1.4), and
            audio switching to the speaker in public when headphones
            disconnect.
      \item \emph{The checklist's prompt to think broadly about safety.} We
            had not seen a language app as something that could harm
            wellbeing, but the checklist made us look at photosensitivity
            (H2.4), explicit lyrics without warning (H3.2), and streaks and
            notifications that create guilt (H3.3). Authentic songs often
            contain profanity, and a learner who repeats a slur without
            knowing its register is a real harm we had missed.
      \item \emph{Treating song maps as untrusted input.} Once we accepted that
            a map is a file anyone can edit and share, security hazards
            followed: crafted maps (H4.3), external URLs (H4.4), and paths
            that escape the package (H4.5). Reading the AnkiConnect
            documentation to check our claims showed that it listens only
            on \texttt{127.0.0.1} by default, which made us realize that our
            own setup instructions could be the thing that opens it to the
            network (H6.5). Checking the boundary for every input also
            reminded us that the libraries we depend on are an input as well
            (H7.3).
    \end{itemize}
    The main lesson for us is that most of the hazards were not about the
    software failing. They exist when the software works exactly as
    designed, which is why we would not have found them by thinking only
    about bugs.

    \item \textbf{Other than the risk of physical harm (some projects may not
    have any appreciable risks of this form), list at least 2 other types of
    risk in software products. Why are they important to consider?}\\
    \emph{Security and privacy risk:} software can expose or misuse personal
    data, or let an attacker run code or change data. For \progname{}, a
    shared song map is untrusted input and the app writes into the learner's
    Anki collection, so a flaw could damage data the learner has built up
    over years. These risks matter because users usually cannot see them and
    the harm is often permanent.

    \emph{Psychological and wellbeing risk:} engagement mechanics such as
    streaks and notifications can create guilt, stress, or compulsive use,
    and offensive content without warning can upset users. These risks
    matter because the people most affected, such as young or vulnerable
    users, are least able to recognize them, and they grow with the number
    of users.

    \emph{Legal and content accuracy risk:} distributing unlicensed content or
    teaching incorrect information can cause legal liability or lasting
    harm to users who trust the product. For a learning tool, incorrect
    content is the main way it can fail its users even when the software
    works perfectly.
\end{enumerate}
